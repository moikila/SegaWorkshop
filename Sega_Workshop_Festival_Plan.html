Sega Workshop

A participatory introduction to Mauritian music through rhythm, voice and ensemble playing.

So, what is this (in short)?

A structured, beginner-friendly activity that promotes Mauritian music culture through participation. Each group learns the sega pulse, adds a simple vocal response, develops three instrumental parts and finishes with a genuine ensemble performance.

3workshops across the festival day
30 minfrom welcome to final performance
15active places in each workshop
1:5teacher-to-participant station ratio
How it works

Learn together then perform

The lead teaches the shared pulse and vocal response. Participants then move into ravanne, djembe and support-instrument groups before re-forming as one ensemble.

Instrument model

1 x Ravannne Station, 1 x Djembe Station and 1 x Various instrument station

Provide five ravannes and five djembes. The support station uses five parts selected from maravanne, cajón, sticks and triangle.

Who it is for

Ages 9 to adult

No musical experience or Mauritian Creole is required. Children under 12 should remain with a responsible adult, subject to festival policy.

Operating principle

Listen together play together stop together

This is a guided music lesson—not an unrestricted noise-making activity. Everyone follows shared cues and plays an agreed part.

Section 2 of 10

Concept and cultural framing

The design borrows a strong idea from Pub Choir: teach achievable parts separately, remove the need for musical literacy and bring everyone together for a satisfying shared result. It also foregrounds musicality—hearing and feeling rhythm before playing it.

Objectives

  • Promote Mauritian musical culture through active participation rather than passive demonstration.
  • Introduce the sound, role and respectful handling of ravanne, maravanne and triangle.
  • Help participants feel the rolling sega pulse as two main beats divided into three: ONE two three, TWO two three.
  • Use a short call-and-response chorus or neutral vocable so people can join without speaking Mauritian Creole.
  • Teach one achievable part per instrument and finish with a complete ensemble performance.
  • Encourage participants to seek out Mauritian artists, music and community events.

Cultural framing

Traditional Mauritian Sega Tipik is recognised by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage and is closely associated with the Creole community. Ravanne, maravanne and triangle are the core traditional instruments. Djembe is used here as an advanced workshop instrument and cajón may be used in the support station; both should be described as percussion adaptations rather than traditional Sega Tipik instruments.

Open with a 60–90 second, age-appropriate introduction covering where sega comes from, its connection to Mauritian Creole history and community life, and the relationship between song, rhythm and dance.

Language: an invented phrase should not be presented as authentic Mauritian Creole. Use a genuine, cleared refrain with agreed pronunciation and meaning, or clearly describe a neutral vocable as a workshop device.

The workshop is

  • A guided, hands-on music lesson
  • A respectful cultural introduction
  • A collaborative ensemble experience
  • Beginner-friendly and learn-by-ear
  • Structured around listening and shared cues

The workshop is not

  • An unrestricted noise-making zone
  • A claim of authentic mastery in 30 minutes
  • A competition or musical test
  • Dependent on notation or Creole
  • Continuous playing while teachers speak

Capacity and booking

  • Advertise 15 active places per workshop: three instrument groups of five and up to 45 places across the day.
  • Count every actively participating adult or child as one place, including an adult joining to support a younger child.
  • Use advance booking where possible, with a small walk-up allocation or waitlist.
  • Issue a coloured session card or wristband so participants can return at the correct time.
  • Close entry once the safety briefing starts. Late arrivals may observe but should not receive instruments mid-session.
  • Set the final cap by safe, playable instrument numbers—not floor space alone.

Participation agreement

At the start, ask everyone to agree that instruments remain quiet when the stop signal is raised, they will play only the demonstrated pattern, and all instruments will be handled with care.

Section 4 of 10

The 30-minute session

Move from listening to shared pulse, voice, small-group coaching and finally a complete performance. Reliability matters more than complexity.

  1. 0–2 minutesWelcome, context and playing agreement

    Introduce Sega Tipik, name the instruments and establish the stop signal.

  2. 2–5 minutesListen and find the pulse

    Use a live duo or approved track; pat knees or step before handing out instruments.

  3. 5–9 minutesSticks and triangle foundation

    Echo short patterns; count ONE two three, TWO two three; practise start and stop.

  4. 9–12 minutesAdd the vocal response

    Teach a short chorus or neutral vocable by call and response, then combine it with the pulse.

  5. 12–14 minutesMove into three stations

    Use pre-assigned colours. Participants carry no instruments between stations.

  6. 14–24 minutesInstrument coaching

    Ravanne, djembe and support-instrument groups learn one repeating part.

  7. 24–26 minutesRe-form the ensemble

    The lead sets tempo and each section demonstrates its pattern once.

  8. 26–28 minutesTwo guided run-throughs

    First instruments only, then instruments plus vocal response.

  9. 28–30 minutesFinal performance and close

    Perform without interruption, thank participants and name the instruments again.

Core facilitation cues

  • Teach sound before terminology.
  • Use the same stop, count-in and ending cues at every station.
  • Build from body pulse to foundation instrument, then allocated station instrument.
  • If time is lost, remove one layer and rebuild.

Song and track brief

  • Clear, steady sega feel with audible triple subdivision
  • Comfortable first-timer tempo
  • Two-to-three-minute final arrangement
  • Short vocal response with easy vowels
  • Obvious introduction and planned ending
  • Offline copies on two devices and a live fallback

Confirm public-performance and recording rights with the festival before rehearsals. Use an original or explicitly cleared arrangement, or confirm the organiser’s OneMusic coverage.

Section 5 of 10

Instrument teaching briefs

Each station uses the same ten-minute arc: identify the instrument, establish safe posture, say and practise the sounds slowly, loop a short pattern, then rehearse the shared cues.

Shared foundation with sticks and triangle
  • Name the instruments and demonstrate quiet ready position before distribution.
  • Establish the two main pulses and groups of three using voice and knee pats.
  • Model a simple repeated pattern, alternating leader and group echoes.
  • Practise count-in, stop signal and a soft dynamic before music.
  • Keep sticks below shoulder height; triangle beaters point down at rest.
Advanced station — ravanne
  1. 0–1: identify the central frame drum of Sega Tipik and explain respectful handling.
  2. 1–3: show supported grip, posture and safe hand position; offer a stand if needed.
  3. 3–5: demonstrate the one or two sounds required, using shared syllables.
  4. 5–8: teach a one-bar loop by echo, slowly and then at performance tempo.
  5. 8–10: practise count-in, four repeats, stop and restart. Never heat animal-skin heads with an open flame in the public area.
Advanced station — djembe
  1. 0–1: identify the djembe as an advanced workshop instrument and a supporting adaptation.
  2. 1–3: show safe posture, stable placement and relaxed hands.
  3. 3–5: teach only the low and high sound needed, or simplify to one sound.
  4. 5–8: build the repeated pattern one pulse at a time.
  5. 8–10: practise count-in, staying beneath ravanne volume, stop and restart.
Support station — maravanne, cajón, sticks or triangle
  1. 0–1: name the allocated instrument and its role. Identify maravanne and triangle as traditional; describe cajón and sticks as workshop tools.
  2. 1–3: demonstrate safe grip or posture and the one required sound or motion.
  3. 3–5: establish the simplest subdivision or accent using the shared count.
  4. 5–8: loop for four to eight cycles and practise rejoining after losing the pulse.
  5. 8–10: practise count-in, playing under the vocal response, stop and restart.

Teaching video specification

Record one 60–90 second reference video for each part and one combined ensemble video. Show the instrument name and role, grip from front and side, the pattern slowly with spoken sounds, four clean cycles at performance tempo, and the shared count-in, stop signal and ending.

Instructor readiness

  • Hold a current Victorian Working with Children Clearance or organiser-accepted exemption where required.
  • Complete the festival child-safety briefing and understand reporting contacts.
  • Teach in an open group area and avoid isolated one-to-one contact.
  • Know the part, sound syllables, cues, ending and simplified fallback.

One teaching cycle

Say it → tap it → play it slowly → loop it → add the music.

  • Start with one basic action.
  • Use call and response.
  • Loop for four to eight cycles.
  • Teach participants how to rejoin.
  • Add one layer at a time.
  • Simplify early when needed.
WWC Connect: because the festival occurs after 19 October 2026, the engaging organisation should verify its WWC Connect obligations if instructors are engaged in child-related work. Current VIT-registered teachers should follow the separate notification rules that apply.

Minimum rehearsal plan

  1. Music meeting to agree culture, song, pulse language, vocal response, parts, tempo and ending.
  2. Each teacher delivers the ten-minute station lesson to the others.
  3. Run the full 30 minutes with a visible timer and both transitions.
  4. Pilot with 10–15 invited beginners and simplify weak instructions or patterns.
  5. Run a final production rehearsal with the exact layout, audio, instruments and roles.

Go or no-go: retain the 15-person format only if a pilot group reaches the final performance by minute 28 without teachers playing every part for them.

Section 7 of 10

Space and equipment

Plan for a weather-protected area of roughly 8 × 8 metres or larger, confirmed on site. Use one central semicircle and three marked clusters of five armless chairs.

Layout checklist

  • Maintain accessible paths, clear sightlines to the lead and a buffer from neighbouring amplified activities.
  • Separate the ravanne and djembe groups so both teachers can be heard without competing on volume.
  • Place the speaker in front of participants and away from registration.
  • Use station colours on chairs and instruments for fast allocation and return.
  • Provide an observer area that does not block exits, paths or the view of children.
  • Confirm shade, rain and wind plans, power, cables, lighting, toilets, water and first aid.

Accessibility measures

  • Provide a step-free route and at least one flexible space for a wheelchair or mobility aid.
  • Offer seated, standing and lower-impact ways to keep the pulse; never require dancing or singing.
  • Combine demonstrations, spoken counts and visual cue cards.
  • Offer an edge seat or quieter location and keep volume suitable for instruction.
  • Adapt grip or support instruments on a safe stand or table.
  • Invite access needs at registration without unnecessary medical detail.
